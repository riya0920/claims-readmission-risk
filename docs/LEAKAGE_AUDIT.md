## 1. The obvious leak, priced

`discharge_status` is the UB-04 patient-status field. It is populated
at discharge coding, which means it is downstream of the thing we are
predicting. Here is what it buys:

| model | AUROC | sensitivity @ top 5% | PPV @ top 5% |
|---|---|---|---|
| features as specified | 0.6467 | 11.6% | 38.5% |
| + discharge_status | 0.9040 | 30.1% | 100.0% |
| **difference** | **+0.2572** | **+18.5%** | **+61.5%** |

Readmission rate by disposition code, which is why:

| code | meaning | readmission rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ? | 6.2% |
| 3 | ? | 100.0% |
| 6 | ? | 17.1% |
| 62 | Inpatient rehabilitation facility | 0.0% |
| 7 | ? | 100.0% |

The lift is large enough to look like a good week's work, which is
exactly the problem. Nothing about the number tells you it is wrong;
only knowing when the field is populated does. This is why the
policy is structural (`features.py` never offers the column) rather
than a note in a README.

## 3. The non-obvious leak: claim runout

No feature here uses the future. Every one is computed from claims with
service dates before the index admission. The leak is that a warehouse
snapshot contains claims that **had not arrived yet** at scoring time.

Median receipt lag in this dataset is ~41 days for facility claims and
~12 days for professional claims. So the same feature name means two
different things:

| feature | mean at serving time | mean with complete history | understated by |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ed_visits_90d` | 0.16 | 0.29 | 44% |
| `ed_visits_365d` | 1.07 | 1.20 | 11% |
| `ip_admits_365d` | 0.58 | 0.65 | 11% |
| `ip_days_365d` | 2.88 | 3.24 | 11% |
| `office_visits_90d` | 0.71 | 0.86 | 18% |
| `distinct_providers_180d` | 2.26 | 2.62 | 14% |
| `rx_fills_180d` | 3.36 | 3.39 | 1% |
| `charlson` | 0.75 | 0.76 | 1% |

Recent utilisation is hit hardest, which is the worst possible
place for it, because recency is where the predictive signal is.

### What that costs

| build | evaluated on | AUROC | sens @ top 5% |
|---|---|---|---|
| trained on complete history | complete history | 0.6525 | 11.9% |
| trained on complete history | serving reality | 0.6479 | 11.2% |
| trained on serving reality | serving reality | 0.6467 | 11.6% |

The first row is the number that goes in the notebook. Thesecond is the number the care-management team actually gets.
The measured cost here is **+0.0046 AUROC** and **+0.7% sensitivity at capacity** -- real, in theexpected direction, and smaller than the rhetoric around leakageusually implies. That is worth saying plainly rather than dressingup. Two reasons it is modest in THIS dataset, both of which areproperties of the generator rather than of claims in general:

1. Much of the true signal sits in features that survive runout   largely intact -- comorbidity burden, age, length of stay. A   population whose risk is driven more heavily by recent   utilisation would degrade further.
2. Receipt lag here caps at 120 days. Real payer runout has a   longer tail, and real feeds have outages, retractions, and   adjustment/void pairs that this generator does not emit.

The honest claim is therefore narrow: the skew is real andmeasurable, its size is dataset-specific, and the reason to buildagainst the receipt horizon is not that it recovers a large numberhere -- it is that the size of the gap is UNKNOWABLE unless youmeasure it, and it is not knowable from the notebook that reportsthe first row.

The general rule: **build training features as of the scoring
instant, using the receipt horizon, not the service horizon.** A
point-in-time warehouse (one that can answer 'what did we know on
date D') is the infrastructure that makes this possible, and its
absence is why this failure is common.
